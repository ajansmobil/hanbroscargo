Part Load or Full Load: Which Is Right for Your Move?

Part load or full load?

If your belongings fill only part of a vehicle and your dates are flexible, a part load (groupage) move is usually the more economical choice. If you are moving a whole home, need fixed collection and delivery dates, or prefer your goods not to share a vehicle with other loads, a full load is the better fit.

What the two options mean

Part load (groupage)

In a part load, consignments from different customers travel together in the same vehicle. You pay for the space your belongings use, and the cost of the vehicle is shared across everything on board. In freight terms this is often called LTL (less than truckload). Han Bros Cargo offers part load (groupage) services on its UK and European routes.

Full load

In a full load, the vehicle or container is reserved for you alone. Your goods go from your collection address to your delivery address without any other consignment being added. In freight terms this is known as FTL (full truckload). A smaller dedicated vehicle can also be arranged for lighter moves.

Five questions to help you decide

  1. How much volume do you have? Start with the numbers. Our Smart Calculation tool lets you list your items and see the total cubic metres. If that is a small share of a vehicle, part load comes out ahead.
  2. How flexible are your dates? If you must move into your new home on a set day and receive your goods straight away, a full load makes planning easier. If you can allow a few days either way, part load works well.
  3. How delicate are your belongings? For antiques, a piano or very valuable pieces you may want as little handling as possible. Part loads are professionally packed and secured too, but if minimising handling is the priority, consider a full load.
  4. What matters most in your budget? If cost comes first, part load is likely to suit; if time and control come first, a full load may make more sense.
  5. Will you need storage on arrival? If your new home is not ready yet, sending a part load and holding the goods in storage until delivery can be a sensible option.

Example scenarios

Scenario 1: A student heading to London for a master's degree

For a few boxes of books, clothes and a small desk, reserving a whole vehicle makes no sense. A part load means paying only for the space used.

Scenario 2: A family of four relocating to Germany

With the furniture of a three-bedroom home, white goods and dozens of boxes, the load will fill most of a vehicle. A full load is usually the more sensible choice on both price and timing, and collection and delivery days can be fixed more precisely.

Scenario 3: A couple buying new furniture in Turkey for their home in the Netherlands

A few pieces of factory-fresh furniture can travel well as a part load. They are collected from the manufacturer, their packaging is checked and they leave on a suitable departure. Larger orders may make a full load more attractive.

Scenario 4: An online seller supplying a UK warehouse

Regular but modest commercial shipments are well suited to palletised part loads. As volumes grow, full loads or containers come into play.

Scenario 5: A small business relocating its office

For a small office with a few desks, cabinets, archive boxes and computers, a part load is often enough. If the business must be up and running at its new address on a set date and downtime is costly, however, keeping control of the dates with a full load may work out better overall. The decision should weigh the transport price against the cost of disruption.

Common mistakes

  • Deciding before measuring: “We don't have much” is often wrong. Build your list first.
  • Comparing price alone: when weighing a part load quote against a full load quote, compare delivery timing, packing and insurance as well.
  • Changing at the last minute: discovering in collection week that the volume has grown disrupts the plan. Settle the inventory early.

Other factors that affect the price

The choice between part load and full load is not the only thing that drives the price. When comparing two quotes, make sure each of these is treated the same way in both:

  • Route: origin and destination cities, distance and crossing points all affect cost directly.
  • Access: floor level, lifts, stair width and how close the vehicle can park determine loading and delivery time.
  • Packing: whether materials and labour are included can change the total considerably.
  • Customs: the scope of export and import clearance, and any taxes, should appear as separate items.
  • Insurance: the type of policy and the declared value both affect the price.
  • Extra services: storage, assembly and packaging removal should be listed separately in the quote.

Mixed solutions

Not every move fits neatly into one option. A family that wants to send some belongings straight away and the rest once the new home is ready, for example, might combine two part loads, or a part load with storage. Because each shipment needs its own correct customs paperwork, plans like this should be discussed at the quotation stage.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will my belongings get mixed up with other loads in a part load?

No. Each customer's goods are packed, labelled and tracked against their own inventory. They may share a vehicle, but they are kept separate.

Is a part load slower?

Departure depends on the schedule and how quickly the vehicle fills, so some flexibility is needed. The estimated timeframe is stated in your quote.

At what volume does a full load make sense?

It depends on the route, the dates and the vehicle type. Share your volume with us and we will compare both options in a written quote.

Is a part load insured as well?

Yes. Insurance options are available whichever method you choose, and the cover and terms are confirmed in writing with your quote.

Is customs different between the two?

Either way, your goods are declared in your name. In a part load every consignment on the vehicle is declared separately, so complete paperwork matters.

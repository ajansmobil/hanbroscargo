How to Calculate Cubic Metres (m³) for Your Household Move

How do you calculate cubic metres?

To find the volume of an item, multiply length × width × height; if you measured in centimetres, divide the result by 1,000,000 to convert it to cubic metres (m³). To add up all your belongings, use our Smart Calculation tool and turn your list into a quote request in a single click.

Why does volume matter so much?

In international transport, every vehicle has both a weight limit and a fixed amount of space. Household goods usually take up more space than their weight suggests, so volume plays a decisive role in pricing. As our Smart Calculation page notes, volume and weight are both used to price a move. Knowing your volume in advance helps you choose between a part load and a full load, compare quotes fairly and decide what to take and what to leave.

The basic formula

For a box or any rectangular item:

Volume (m³) = Width (cm) × Depth (cm) × Height (cm) ÷ 1,000,000

If you measure in metres, there is no need to divide: 0.5 m × 0.4 m × 0.4 m = 0.08 m³. One cubic metre is the space taken up by a cube with one-metre sides, and 1 m³ = 1,000,000 cm³ is the basis for every conversion.

Worked examples

The measurements below are examples to show the calculation only; always measure your own items.

Item (example size)CalculationVolume
Box: 50 × 40 × 40 cm50 × 40 × 40 = 80,000 cm³0.08 m³
Sofa: 200 × 90 × 85 cm200 × 90 × 85 = 1,530,000 cm³1.53 m³
Dining table: 160 × 90 × 75 cm160 × 90 × 75 = 1,080,000 cm³1.08 m³
Fridge: 70 × 70 × 185 cm70 × 70 × 185 = 906,500 cm³≈ 0.91 m³

Your total volume is simply the sum of every item. Ten boxes of 0.08 m³ each, for example, come to 0.8 m³.

Tips for measuring accurately

  • Measure the widest points: include sofa arms, table legs and handles in the outer dimensions.
  • Treat odd shapes as boxes: for a corner sofa, a lamp or a bicycle, use the dimensions of an imaginary box that would hold it.
  • Allow for packaging: corner protectors, foam and stretch film add a little to the outer size.
  • Measure dismantled items separately: a wardrobe taken apart into flat panels takes up far less space. Decide first, then measure.
  • Flag items that cannot be stacked: some pieces cannot carry weight on top and effectively use more space than their own volume; mention them when you request a quote.

Are cubic metres the same as volumetric weight?

No. Cubic metres are a direct measure of volume. Volumetric (dimensional) weight is a method used mainly for parcels and courier shipments that converts volume into a weight using a divisor. That divisor varies by transport mode and carrier, and billing is usually based on whichever is higher, actual or volumetric weight. Household removals, by contrast, are generally discussed in cubic metres.

How to estimate weight

Weight matters less than volume for household moves, but it is still taken into account in pricing and vehicle planning. Practical ways to estimate it:

  • With bathroom scales: weigh yourself, then weigh yourself holding the box; the difference is the box's weight. Weighing a few sample boxes and applying the result to similar ones gives a quick estimate.
  • From product information: the weight of white goods and large electronics is listed in the manual or on the manufacturer's website.
  • With Smart Calculation: the weight field is optional; leave it blank and the catalogue value is used. If you know your item's actual weight, enter it to improve the estimate.

Getting ready to measure

A tape measure, a simple spreadsheet and your phone camera are all you need to get the measuring done in an afternoon. Go room by room, note the three dimensions of each item and add a photo. Photos are the quickest way to show exactly what an item is during the survey and when comparing quotes.

Why does the final volume differ from the estimate?

It is common for a homeowner's first estimate to differ from the volume confirmed after a survey. The main reasons are:

  • Forgotten items in the loft, cellar, balcony or inside cupboards
  • The extra size packaging adds to each item
  • Items that cannot be stacked on or laid on their side
  • Last-minute additions in moving week

To keep the gap small, build your list room by room with the cupboards open, and include anything you are undecided about with a note beside it.

Ways to reduce your volume

  • Sell, donate or leave behind things you no longer use before the move.
  • Dismantle furniture wherever possible.
  • Use vacuum bags for clothes and textiles.
  • Fill boxes properly; half-empty boxes waste space and get crushed.
  • Remove anything your new home already has, such as furniture in a furnished let.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I convert centimetres to cubic metres?

Multiply the three measurements in centimetres and divide by 1,000,000. A 50 × 40 × 40 cm box, for example, is 0.08 m³.

Is the Smart Calculation result a final price?

No. The tool gives an initial estimate of volume and weight. The final price depends on the route, the scope of service and the volume confirmed after a survey, and is set out in a written quote.

How many cubic metres is an average room?

It varies enormously with the amount and size of furniture, so an average can be misleading. For an accurate figure, list your items one by one.

Does weight matter too?

Yes. Volume and weight are considered together. For heavy but compact items such as books and tools, weight becomes particularly important.

How do I calculate the volume of dismantled furniture?

Picture the parts as a stack of panels: multiply the length of the longest part, the width of the widest part and the combined thickness of the stacked panels. Remember to allow for packaging.

Do I need to measure every box?

No. Count boxes of the same size, work out the volume of one and multiply by the number of boxes to get the total.

Can I share my list with someone else?

Yes. You can download the list you build in Smart Calculation as a PDF and share it.

Packing Checklist for an International Household Move

How should you pack for an international move?

Good packing for an international move means strong materials, a number on every box that matches the inventory, heavy items in small boxes and fragile pieces wrapped so nothing can shift. Work through the room-by-room checklist below and your belongings will be ready for a long journey and a customs check.

Why is it different from a local move?

On a local move, your belongings are on the road for a few hours. On an international move, boxes travel through very different road conditions, may be re-stowed along the way and can be inspected by customs. Boxes therefore need to be strong and their contents easy to identify. A well-prepared inventory is also the foundation of your customs declaration and of any insurance claim.

Before you start: materials

  • Strong double-wall cartons in a range of sizes
  • Small cartons for books and heavy items
  • Wardrobe cartons for hanging clothes (if possible)
  • Bubble wrap, foam sheets and packing paper
  • Furniture corner protectors and stretch film
  • Wide, strong packing tape and a tape gun
  • Permanent markers, labels and zip bags for screws and fittings
  • A notebook or a spreadsheet on your phone for the inventory

Second-hand supermarket boxes are tempting, but they are rarely strong enough for a long journey. The saving is small compared with the cost of a broken item.

The golden rules

  1. Heavy items in small boxes: books, plates and tools go in small cartons; pillows and bedding go in large ones.
  2. No empty space: contents should not move; fill gaps with paper or textiles.
  3. Number every box: write the number, room and a short description on two sides and add the same details to the inventory.
  4. Do not overfill: boxes with bulging lids get crushed when stacked.
  5. Cross-tape the base: tape the bottom along the centre and the edges so it cannot open in transit.

Room-by-room checklist

Kitchen

  • Wrap plates individually and stand them on edge in the box.
  • Fill glasses with paper and wrap the outside separately.
  • Wrap knives in card so the blades are covered and mark the box.
  • Do not pack opened food, liquids or oils; food can cause problems at customs in many countries.

Living room and bedrooms

  • Dismantle furniture where possible; bag the screws and tape the bag to the matching part.
  • Protect glass and mirrors with corner protectors and foam, and mark them “FRAGILE” and “THIS WAY UP”.
  • Wrap pictures in bubble wrap and sandwich them between two sheets of card.
  • Save space with vacuum bags or wardrobe cartons for clothes.

Electronics

  • Use the original boxes if you still have them.
  • Disconnect and label cables, and photograph the connections.
  • Back up computers and phones, and carry these devices with you if you can.

White goods

  • Fit the transit bolts that lock the washing machine drum; if you no longer have them, ask a service engineer.
  • Empty, defrost and thoroughly dry fridges and freezers before the move.
  • Drain water from dishwasher and washing machine hoses.
  • Secure doors with stretch film rather than tape, which can leave marks.

Bathroom and storage

  • Consider giving away half-used toiletries and cleaning products instead of shipping them.
  • Clean soil from garden tools; soiled items may be checked on entry in some countries.

Special items: pianos, antiques, artwork and rugs

Some items will not fit a standard carton or cannot be protected properly with standard packing. Always leave these to professionals and mention them separately when you ask for a quote:

  • Pianos and large instruments: their weight and delicate mechanisms call for specialist handling equipment and securing. Check doorway and stair clearances in advance.
  • Antique furniture: old timber is sensitive to drying out and knocks, so corners and carved surfaces need extra protection. Photograph existing scratches and cracks before the move.
  • Paintings and mirrors: a made-to-measure crate is the safest option for valuable artwork. Taping a cross over framed glass limits how far pieces scatter if it breaks.
  • Rugs: roll rather than fold rugs that are clean and completely dry, and wrap them in a breathable cover.
  • Chandeliers and lamps: pack bulbs and glass shades separately and wrap the body in foam.

Listing the value of these items on separate inventory lines also helps make sure your insurance cover is set correctly.

What not to pack

Some items should never go on the removal vehicle. Flammable, explosive or pressurised products (gas cylinders, paint, thinners, aerosols, fuel) are refused for safety reasons. We also recommend keeping the following with you:

  • Passports, visas, deeds, certificates and other key documents
  • Cash, jewellery and small valuables
  • Regular medication and prescriptions
  • House keys, laptops and data backups

Check the list of prohibited and restricted goods for your destination country, especially food, plants and seeds, on official sources before you move.

The inventory: the hidden half of packing

The inventory is the list that shows what is in each box. Countries that require a customs declaration, such as the UK, need an English inventory, and the same list supports relief applications such as ToR1. Record the box number, room and a summary of contents on each line, and add an estimated value for valuable pieces. Photographing special items as you pack makes any later claim much easier.

Pack yourself or use professionals?

A popular middle ground is to pack sturdy items such as books and clothes yourself and leave fragile, valuable and bulky pieces to professionals. Han Bros Cargo's professional packing service uses materials suited to each item, including corner protectors for furniture, dedicated boxes for glassware and protective wrapping for electronics, and every box is recorded on the inventory. Bear in mind that boxes you pack yourself may be insured differently, so ask about this when you get your quote.

The first-night box

Pack a separate box for the first days between collection and delivery: a few sets of bedding, towels, basic kitchenware, chargers, a small tool kit, toiletries and some toys for the children. Load it last or take it with you.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should I start packing?

Ideally a few weeks ahead, starting with things you rarely use. Leave everyday items until the last days.

Are boxes I pack myself insured?

Many policies offer more limited cover for owner-packed boxes. Ask for the cover in writing when you receive your quote.

Does the inventory have to be in English?

For shipments to the UK, an English inventory is needed for the customs declaration. Your removals company can prepare it with you.

Do I need to dismantle furniture?

Dismantling furniture where possible reduces volume and lowers the risk of damage. Keep the screws in labelled bags next to the relevant part.

Can I take my plants?

Live plants, seeds and soil are restricted in many countries. Do not ship plants without first checking the destination's rules on official sources.
